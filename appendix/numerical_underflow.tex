\section{The Decoder}\label{sec:num_underflow_decoder}

In this section, we briefly review the decoder, focusing only on the relevant aspects for understanding how numerical underflow arises.
We restrict our discussion to the algorithm for decoding independent bit- or phase-flip noise presented in \cite{efficient_decoding_algorithm}\footnote{
Note that the decoder corrects either bit-flip or phase-flip errors in a single decoding step. 
Consequently, decoding under independent bit- and phase-flip noise is achieved by applying the decoder twice.

Furthermore, there appears to be an error, or at least an inconsistency in the notation, 
in the description of the independent noise model in \cite{efficient_decoding_algorithm}. 
The author assumes independent bit- and phase-flip noise with probabilities $p_X=p_Z$ and subsequently states that $p_Y=p_X^2$. 
However, combining independent bit- and phase-flip channels with probabilities $p_X$ and $p_Z$ results in the effective error probabilities
\[
    \tilde{p}_X = p_X(1-p_Z), \qquad
    \tilde{p}_Z = p_Z(1-p_X), \qquad
    \tilde{p}_Y = p_X p_Z,
\]
where $\tilde{p}$ denotes the probabilities in the combined channel, while $p$ denotes the probabilities of the independent channels.


}, which is the decoder configuration used throughout this thesis 

The algorithm solves the ML decoding problem, see section \ref{ssec:general_ML_decoder}, by explicitly computing the probabilities of the cosets $\text{Pr}(E_\sigma \bar{P}\mathcal{S})$ corresponding to the measured syndrome $\sigma$.
Since  $X$- and $Z$-errors are decoded independently in this algorithm\footnote{
    Note once again that this decoder is only designed to be able to decode either $X$- or $Z$-errors.
} and we only consider a single logical encoded qubit, 
the logical operators defining the two cosets are either $\bar{P} \in \{\bar{I},\bar{X}\}$ or $\bar{P} \in \{\bar{I},\bar{Z}\}$.

In general, the probability of an individual cosets decreases as the code distance increases, since the number of possible syndrome growth exponentially.
For the surface code, the average cosets probability can be expressed as 
\begin{equation}
    \langle \text{Pr}(E_\sigma \bar{P} \mathcal{S}) \rangle = 2^{-m/2}= 2^{-d\cdot(d-1)},
    \label{eq:mean_prop_coset_in_distance}
\end{equation} 
where we consider either $X$- or $Z$-errors, consistent with the assumption of the decoder.
Consequently, we expect the probabilities of individual cosets to become quite small.
Note that we are using a average here an not a median, the median is highly dependent on the physical error rate, as will be discussed later. 

The efficient decoding algorithm consists of two stages. 
First, representative error $E_\sigma$ corresponding to the measured syndrome is generated.
This representative error is then passed to the main part of the decoder, 
which computed the probabilities of the associated cosets.

Broadly speaking, the decoder utilizes a mapping from the surface code to fermionic Gaussian states, 
which are subsequently mapped to matchgate quantum circuits, thereby enabling an efficient computation of the cosets probabilities on classical computers.
The complete derivation and algorithm of this mapping are presented in \cite{efficient_decoding_algorithm}.
For the discussion in this appendix, we point out, that this process included the computation of norm of Gaussian states $\Gamma \geq 0$ and its renormalization.

The algorithm used to generate the conventional representation error $E_\sigma$ is sufficiently short to be quoted in full. 
For the case of $X$-errors \cite{efficient_decoding_algorithm} states:
\begin{quotation}
``Let [$\sigma_u$] be the syndrome[bit] of a site stabilizer $A_u$. 
We choose the desired error [$E_\sigma$] by connecting each site $u$ with a non-zero syndrome [$\sigma_u$] to the left boundary by a horizontal string of $X$-errors 
and adding all such strings modulo two. 
Note that [$E_\sigma$] can be constructed in time $\mathcal{O}(n)$.'' 
\end{quotation}
Here, we adapted the notation to match the notation used throughout this thesis. 
We refer to this method to construct the representative error as the \emph{Bravyi} approach.


\section{The Problem}\label{sec:num_underflow_the_problem}

Before the rejection rate was implemented as a mitigation strategy, the logical error rate exhibited the form shown in \ref{fig:exmaple_observed_numerical_underflow}.
The simulation was performed using code-capacity noise model with only bit-flip errors, measuring the corresponding logical $Z$ observable.
The logical error rate $p_{log}$ generally follows the expected behavior for high physical error rates $p_{phy}$.
In particular the logical error rate curves for the code distances $d\in \{3,5,7\}$, show no obvious anomaly and they exhibit the asymptotic scaling predicted by equation \ref{eq:p_log_asymptotic_behavior}, as discussed in section \ref{ssec:threshold_theorem}.

Starting with the logical error rate curve of a distance $d=9$ surface code, an anomalous upward trend becomes apparent.
We can observe that the logical error rate exhibits an upward trend, 
breaking from the expected asymptotic scaling, at a physical error rates of $p_{phy} \approx 5\%$.
We will refer to this point as the \emph{critical point}.
Below the critical point, the logical error rate exhibits large fluctuations between different physical error rates, together with an overall slight upward trend as the physical noise rate decreases.
Considering the higher distances, we observe that the critical point is shifting to higher noise rates as the code distance is increased. 

Interestingly, the irregularity of the logical error rate curves below the critical point appears to not be random. 
Repeating the simulation yield the same curve, up to the expected statistical uncertainty. 
We therefore conclude that the observed anomaly is deterministic, rather than a statistical artifact.

As shown in equation \ref{eq:mean_prop_coset_in_distance}, the average probability of an individual coset decreases with increasing code distance.
Likewise, reducing the physical error rate decreases the probability of most cosets, since the trivial (no-error) coset becomes increasingly dominant. 
This reduction in coset probability shows the same quantitative behavior as the observed anomaly and the shift of its critical point.  
Consequently, the probabilities involved in the decoding algorithm become progressively smaller, suggesting numerical underflow as a plausible explanation for the observed anomaly.
Since numerical underflow is itself a deterministic effect, this is consistent with the reproducibility of the anomalous logical error-rate curves.

It is worth noting that the anomaly appears to occur well below the threshold of this noise model.
However, without understanding the underlying mechanism, it it not possible to determine whether the anomaly already affects the results at higher physical noise levels.
As numerical underflow could occur at small enough scales not being visible, but still influencing the results. 
The next section therefore introduces error mitigation and detection approaches.

\section{Mitigation Strategies}\label{sec:num_underflow_solutions}

In the following, we present three different strategies to mitigate the numerical underflow, 
either by reducing its occurrence or by limiting its impact on the resulting logical error rate curves.

Of the three approaches discussed below, only the logarithm and rejection rate strategy was implemented in the final simulation,
resulting in the rejection rates shown in all logical error rate figures. 

\subsection{Precision}
% increase precision  -> use better suited programming language
Assuming that the observed anomaly is indeed caused by a numerical underflow problem, the most straightforward mitigation strategy is to increase the numerical precision. 
Unfortunately, parts of our simulations framework\footnote{
    This limitation is largely due to the initial choice of programming language.
    As this implementation is written in \texttt{Python}, and relies on \texttt{NumPy} \cite{numpy} together with \texttt{Numba} \cite{numba} to just in time compile to achieve acceptable performance. 
    Although \texttt{NumPy} provides the the high precision floating point data type \texttt{numpy.float128}, 
    this type is not fully supported throughout the relevant parts of \texttt{NumPy} itself and \texttt{Numba}.
    This in combination with general slower performance of Python, motivates a reimplementation, of the simulation done in this thesis, in a different programming language, for example \texttt{C++}.
    Sadly the time for the required reimplementation was not there, yet.
} do not support higher precision floating point arithmetic. 
We therefore consider alternative mitigation strategies. 

\subsection{Logarithm and Rejection Rate}
% calculated the logarithm of the probability instead of the probability  (arons idea)

The susceptibility of the implementation can be reduced by calculating the logarithm of the coset probability\footnote{
    Aron Marton provided the idea for this solution, as he encountered similar problems in his own implementation.
} instead of the probability itself.
This strategy is applied to both the computation of the probability and to the computation of the norm of the Gaussian state. 

The computation of the norm of the Gaussian state requires the evaluation of the determinant of a matrix.
Without going into the details of the algorithm, this determinant is computed for a sparse matrix whose entries become increasingly small as the physical noise rate decreases.
Even when using a function specifically designed to compute the logarithm of determinant, in our case we used \texttt{numpy.linalg.slogdet}, 
numerical underflow is still observed to occur. 
In these cases the norm $\Gamma$, which should satisfy $\Gamma \geq 0$, becomes negative.

As negative value of the norm $\Gamma$ is therefore used as an indicator that numerical underflow has occurred. 
Whenever this condition is detected the decoding process is flagged as invalid.
All simulation shots containing a decoding event which is flagged as invalid, are discarded, 
giving rise to the rejection rate $r_{\mathrm{rejection}}$ 
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{rejection}} = n_{\mathrm{rejection}}/n_{shots},
\end{equation}
which is plotted alongside all logical error rate curves\footnote{
    Only the maximal rejection rate over all distances is plotted, as this is the upper bound as how much the resulting threshold might be influenced by the rejection of shots. 
}, to indicate the susceptibility to numerical underflow at a given physical error rate. 

The combination of logarithmic calculation and rejection rate strategy yield logical error rate curves that exhibit the expected asymptotic scaling, 
as shown in figure \ref{fig:results_code_capcity_Z_ml_p_log_p_phy_slopes}. 

Note that the rejection rate strategy, in combination with assigning a fixed representative error for each syndrome, results in the deterministic rejection of certain syndromes. 
Since each syndrome is always mapped to the same representative error, any syndrome whose representative errors leads to numerical underflow in the main part of the decoder\footnote{
    We observed that a different representative error of the same coset can lead to no numerical underflow.
    The formulation of this as a mitigation strategy is done in the next section \ref{ssec:numerical_underflow_representative_error}.
} is rejected in every simulation.

It seems that there is no relation between the probability of a successful recovery\footnote{
    The relation between successful recovery probability and rejection probability, would be an interesting topic to look into, 
    if one would want to use the efficient ML decoder without solving the numerical underflow, only mitigating its effect by the rejection approach. 
} for a given centralizer and the rejection probability of the corresponding syndrome. 
To be sure we try to limit the rejection rate of shots, by using low code distances, accepting some finite size effects, in the determination of the threshold.

\subsection{Representative Error}\label{ssec:numerical_underflow_representative_error}
% change the representative error algorithm -> pre decoding with mwpm 
%   note that arons implementation intrinsicly used this -> thus leading to the less 
A further reduction in the occurrence of the numerical underflow can be achieved by using a representative error with higher probability of occurrence.
This observation arose while comparing Aron Marton's implementation with the implementation used in this thesis.
Since exchanging the main decoder part did not change the numerical underflow occurrences significantly, 
we concluded that the choice of representative error construction method is relevant to the numerical underflow. 
Whereas this thesis follows the representative error construction algorithm proposed in \cite{efficient_decoding_algorithm},
Aron Marton's utilized the MWPM decoder to generate representative error. 

The Bravyi representative error construction method introduced in section \ref{sec:num_underflow_decoder} and propose in \cite{efficient_decoding_algorithm} 
often produces representative errors with relatively high weight. 
For example, a single physical error occurring on the right side of the surface code, results in a representative error of weight up to $2(d-1)$, since both triggered stabilizers are connected to the left boundary.
Consequently, particularly at low physical error rates and for large code distances, the representative error may have a much larger weight than the physical error that actually occurred.

In contrast, using a MWPM decoder to construct the representative error, yields a significantly lower weight representative error.
Owing to the iterative structure of the decoding algorithm \cite{efficient_decoding_algorithm}, this reduces the probability of numerical underflow\footnote{
    We are able to pin-point the calculation, leading to numerical underflow, 
    but to be able to clearly state in which optimal occurrence probability the representative error has is in detail more complex than the single statement above. 
    Therefore the statement would benefit from more research.
}. 

It should be noted, that the construction approaches are not directly equivalent.
The Bravyi approach constructs a representative error belonging to the logically trivial coset $\bar{P} = \bar{I}$.
In contrast, the MWPM approach returns the lowest weight error from coset of the centralizer with the corresponding syndrome, see section \ref{ssec:intro_centralizer} for a detailed discussion of the centralizer.
Consequently, additional processing is required after the MWPM decoding, as introduced in section \ref{ssec:general_MWMP_decoder}, to obtain an equivalent representative error construction method.

Specifically, the logical coset to which the representative error belongs must be identified, for the MWPM case. 
If necessary a logical operator is then applied, such that the resulting error belongs to the trivial coset. 
This process\footnote{
    One could possibly reformulate the MWPM algorithm, 
    It may be possible to reformulate the MWPM algorithm, such that only errors belonging to the trivial coset are constructed.
    investigating such a modification is interesting, however, beyond the scope of this thesis.
} adds at most the weight $d$ to the weight of the representative error for the surface code, 
under code-capacity noise if either bit- or phase-flip errors are decoded, which is the case in this thesis.

To obtain a conservative upper bound on the representative error weight, 
we consider a modified data set in which the worst case additional weight is added to every MWPM representative error.
We refer to this data set by \emph{MWPM+d}.

The difference in the representative error weight and corresponding probability is shown in figure \cref{fig:numerical_underflow_avg_weight,fig:numerical_underflow_avg_prob,fig:numerical_underflow_weight_distr}. 
All results are based on a distance $d=15$ surface code, with $100k$ sampled syndromes under circuit level noise, with only bit-flip noise.

Figure \ref{fig:numerical_underflow_avg_weight} shows the average representative error weight over a relevant interval of physical error rates.
A clear difference between the two construction methods can be observed: 
The representative error constructed by the MWPM approach have on average, only about half of the weight of those generated by the Bravyi approach.
The corresponding average probabilities of each approach can be seen in figure \ref{fig:numerical_underflow_avg_prob}.
As expected, the lower representative-error weights obtained with the MWPM approach translate into significantly higher representative-error probabilities.
The same observation holds for the worst case data set `MWPM+d', with a slight reduction in the difference, but still being significant.

This difference is not only limited to average values. 
Figure \ref{fig:numerical_underflow_weight_distr} shows the weight distribution of the representative error over all sampled syndromes
at the physical noise rate $p_{phy}=10\%$. 
Even the smallest weight representative errors of the Bravyi approach are only comparable to the largest weight MWPM approach in their weight.

From a mathematical perspective, the efficient ML decoder \cite{efficient_decoding_algorithm} can compute the coset probability using any error belonging to the corresponding coset. 
Consequently the choice of representative error construction method can be made based on details properties relevant to the implementation. 
Shown in this chapter are two method which can be placed on different ends of the complexity versus error weight scale.
The MWPM approach returns by definition the error with the minium weight and therefore the highest likelihood of occurring, at an increased classical computational overhead.
In contrast the method proposed by Bravyi has low computational overhead but results in a high weight error. 
The exact choice which approach is the optimal depends on the requirements of the implementation.

\subsection{Short Conclusion}

Given the time constraints of this thesis, we chose to implement the logarithmic calculation and rejection strategy as the techniques for mitigating numerical underflow.

Since the combination of logarithmic calculations and the rejection strategy proved sufficient to suppress the numerical underflow in the physical noise rate regime relevant to this work, 
we did not implement the MWPM based representative error construction method, 
or reimplemented the decoder in a programming language supporting higher precision and improved computational performance. 

For a future implementation, reimplementing the decoder in such a programming language appears to be the most promising approach, 
particular when combined with the logarithmic calculation approach.
Retaining the rejection strategy is also beneficial, as it provides a direct measure of the occurrence of numerical underflow 
and therefore serves as a useful diagnostic tool before numerical underflow begins to affect the simulation results.

Finally, the choice of representative error construction method should depend on the specific needs. 
If higher-precision arithmetic together with logarithmic calculations is sufficient to eliminate numerical underflow, 
the computational overhead of the MWPM preprocessing step can be avoided. 
However, for large code distances, the MWPM-based representative-error construction may substantially reduce the rejection rate, 
making large-scale simulations more efficient.
